# The Human Heart: Structure, Function and Medical History

## Size and position

The heart is a muscular organ about the size of a closed fist. In an adult it usually weighs between 250 and 350 grams. It sits in the middle of the chest in a space called the mediastinum, between the two lungs, with about two thirds of its mass lying to the left of the midline. The pointed lower tip of the heart is called the apex, and it points down and to the left.

The heart is wrapped in a double-walled sac called the pericardium. A thin layer of fluid between the two walls of the sac lets the heart move without friction as it beats. The wall of the heart itself has three layers. The outer layer is the epicardium. The thick middle layer, made of cardiac muscle, is the myocardium. The smooth inner lining that touches the blood is the endocardium.

## The four chambers

The heart has four chambers. The two upper chambers are the right atrium and the left atrium, and they receive blood coming back to the heart. The two lower chambers are the right ventricle and the left ventricle, and they pump blood out. A wall called the septum separates the right side from the left side so that blood from the two sides does not mix.

The wall of the left ventricle is the thickest part of the heart, about 8 to 12 millimetres in a healthy adult, because it has to push blood through the whole body. The wall of the right ventricle is only about a third as thick, since it only pumps blood the short distance to the lungs, where the pressure is much lower.

## The four valves

Four valves keep blood moving in one direction. The tricuspid valve lies between the right atrium and the right ventricle. The pulmonary valve lies between the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery. The mitral valve, which has two flaps and is also called the bicuspid valve, lies between the left atrium and the left ventricle. The aortic valve lies between the left ventricle and the aorta.

The tricuspid and mitral valves are held in place by thin, strong cords called chordae tendineae, which are attached to small papillary muscles on the ventricle walls. These cords stop the valve flaps from being pushed backward into the atria when the ventricles contract. The familiar "lub-dub" sound of the heartbeat comes from the valves closing. The first sound is made by the mitral and tricuspid valves closing, and the second by the aortic and pulmonary valves closing.

## The path of blood

Blood that has given up its oxygen to the body returns to the right atrium through two large veins, the superior vena cava and the inferior vena cava. It passes through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle, which pumps it through the pulmonary valve into the pulmonary artery and on to the lungs. This loop between the heart and the lungs is called the pulmonary circulation.

In the lungs the blood picks up oxygen and releases carbon dioxide. It returns to the left atrium through four pulmonary veins, passes through the mitral valve into the left ventricle, and is pumped through the aortic valve into the aorta. The aorta is the largest artery in the body, about 2.5 centimetres wide where it leaves the heart. The loop from the heart to the body and back is called the systemic circulation.

The heart muscle has its own blood supply. Two coronary arteries branch off the aorta just above the aortic valve. The left main coronary artery splits into the left anterior descending artery and the circumflex artery. The right coronary artery supplies the right side of the heart and, in most people, the bottom of the left ventricle. A blocked coronary artery causes a heart attack, known in medicine as a myocardial infarction.

## The electrical system

The heart beats without being told to by the brain. Each beat starts in a small group of cells in the wall of the right atrium called the sinoatrial node, which is the heart's natural pacemaker. Left to itself, the sinoatrial node fires between 60 and 100 times per minute.

The electrical signal spreads across both atria and makes them contract. It then reaches the atrioventricular node, which holds the signal back for about 0.1 seconds. This short delay gives the ventricles time to fill with blood. The signal then travels down the bundle of His, splits into the left and right bundle branches, and spreads through the ventricles along the Purkinje fibers, so that the ventricles contract from the apex upward.

The electrical activity can be recorded from the skin as an electrocardiogram. A normal trace shows a P wave as the atria are activated, a QRS complex as the ventricles are activated, and a T wave as the ventricles recover. The Dutch doctor Willem Einthoven built the first practical electrocardiograph using a string galvanometer and won the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1924 for it.

## Numbers that describe the pump

A resting adult heart beats about 100,000 times a day. With each beat the left ventricle pushes out about 70 millilitres of blood, an amount called the stroke volume. Multiplying the stroke volume by the heart rate gives the cardiac output, which is about 5 litres per minute at rest. During hard exercise the cardiac output of a trained athlete can rise above 30 litres per minute.

The ventricle does not empty completely with each beat. The share of blood that it pushes out is called the ejection fraction, and a normal value for the left ventricle is between 55 and 70 percent. Doctors usually measure it with an ultrasound scan called an echocardiogram.

The phase in which the ventricles contract is called systole, and the phase in which they relax and fill is called diastole. Blood pressure is written as the pressure during systole over the pressure during diastole. A typical healthy reading is 120 over 80 millimetres of mercury.

The heart adjusts its output on its own. According to the Frank-Starling law, the more the ventricle is stretched by incoming blood, the harder it contracts on the next beat. This keeps the output of the right and left sides in balance.

## The heart before birth

A baby in the womb gets its oxygen from the placenta, not from its lungs, so most blood skips the lungs. Two shortcuts make this possible. The foramen ovale is an opening in the wall between the right and left atria. The ductus arteriosus is a short vessel that joins the pulmonary artery to the aorta. Both normally close soon after birth, when the baby starts to breathe. The closed foramen ovale leaves a small dent called the fossa ovalis.

## Milestones in heart medicine

The English doctor William Harvey was the first to describe correctly how blood circulates. He published his findings in 1628 in a book usually known by its short title, De Motu Cordis. Before Harvey, most doctors followed the ancient writer Galen and believed that blood was made in the liver and used up by the body.

In 1929 a young German doctor, Werner Forssmann, pushed a thin tube through a vein in his own arm into his heart and took an X-ray to prove it. This was the first cardiac catheterization, and it earned him a share of the Nobel Prize in 1956.

On May 6, 1953 the American surgeon John Gibbon carried out the first successful open-heart operation using a heart-lung machine. The patient was an 18-year-old woman named Cecelia Bavolek, and the machine took over the work of her heart and lungs for 26 minutes.

The first fully implantable pacemaker was put into a patient in Sweden on October 8, 1958. The surgeon was Åke Senning and the device was designed by the engineer Rune Elmqvist. The patient, Arne Larsson, went through 26 pacemakers during his life and died in 2001 at the age of 86, having outlived both the surgeon and the engineer.

The first human-to-human heart transplant was performed by Christiaan Barnard on December 3, 1967 at Groote Schuur Hospital in Cape Town, South Africa. The patient, Louis Washkansky, lived for 18 days before dying of pneumonia. Survival improved greatly after the drug cyclosporine, which stops the body from rejecting the new organ, came into wide use in the early 1980s.

## Common terms for rhythm problems

A resting heart rate above 100 beats per minute is called tachycardia, and one below 60 beats per minute is called bradycardia. Atrial fibrillation is the most common long-lasting rhythm problem. In it the atria quiver in a fast, disorganized way and do not contract properly, which raises the risk of blood clots and stroke. Ventricular fibrillation is far more dangerous: the ventricles quiver and pump no blood, and the person dies within minutes unless a defibrillator delivers an electric shock to reset the rhythm.
