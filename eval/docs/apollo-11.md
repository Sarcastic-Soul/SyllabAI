# Apollo 11: The First Crewed Moon Landing

## Background and goal

Apollo 11 was the fifth crewed flight of NASA's Apollo program and the first to put people on the Moon. The goal it met had been set by President John F. Kennedy in a speech to Congress on May 25, 1961, when he asked the nation to land a man on the Moon and return him safely to Earth before the end of the decade. At the time of that speech the United States had only fifteen minutes of human spaceflight experience, from Alan Shepard's suborbital flight three weeks earlier.

The Apollo flights before it tested the hardware in steps. Apollo 7 checked the command and service module in Earth orbit. Apollo 8 took a crew around the Moon for the first time in December 1968. Apollo 9 tested the lunar module in Earth orbit, and Apollo 10 flew a full dress rehearsal that brought its lunar module to within about 15.6 kilometres of the lunar surface without landing.

## The crew

Three astronauts flew the mission, and all three had flown in the Gemini program before. Neil Armstrong was the commander. Michael Collins was the command module pilot and stayed in lunar orbit while the other two went down to the surface. Edwin "Buzz" Aldrin was the lunar module pilot. The backup crew was Jim Lovell, William Anders and Fred Haise.

The crew chose the names of their two spacecraft. The command module was called Columbia and the lunar module was called Eagle, after the bald eagle on the mission patch. The patch was unusual because it did not carry the names of the crew; Collins, who designed it, wanted it to stand for everyone who had worked on the landing.

## The Saturn V rocket

Apollo 11 was launched by a Saturn V rocket with the serial number SA-506. The Saturn V stood 110.6 metres tall and weighed about 2.9 million kilograms when fully fuelled. It had three stages. The first stage, called the S-IC, was built by Boeing and used five F-1 engines burning RP-1 kerosene and liquid oxygen. Together the five engines produced about 34.5 meganewtons of thrust at liftoff. The first stage burned for roughly 2 minutes and 41 seconds and lifted the rocket to an altitude of about 68 kilometres.

The second stage, the S-II, was built by North American Aviation and used five J-2 engines burning liquid hydrogen and liquid oxygen. The third stage, the S-IVB, was built by the Douglas Aircraft Company and had a single J-2 engine that could be restarted in space. The third stage fired twice: once to reach a parking orbit around Earth, and a second time for the translunar injection burn that sent the spacecraft toward the Moon. That second burn lasted just under six minutes.

## Launch and the trip to the Moon

The rocket lifted off from Launch Complex 39A at the Kennedy Space Center in Florida on July 16, 1969 at 13:32 UTC. About one million people watched from the roads and beaches near the launch site. Twelve minutes after liftoff the spacecraft was in Earth orbit, and after one and a half orbits the third stage sent it on its way to the Moon.

Shortly after leaving Earth orbit, Collins separated Columbia from the third stage, turned it around, and docked with Eagle, which was stored in an adapter on top of the stage. This step was called transposition and docking. The joined spacecraft then coasted for about three days. On July 19 the crew fired the service module's main engine behind the Moon to slow down and enter lunar orbit. They went on to circle the Moon thirty times in total.

## The landing

On July 20, Armstrong and Aldrin moved into Eagle and separated from Columbia. During the powered descent the lunar module's guidance computer showed several program alarms with the codes 1202 and 1201. These alarms meant the computer was being asked to do more work than it had time for and was dropping low-priority tasks. In Mission Control, the 26-year-old guidance officer Steve Bales, supported by the software specialist Jack Garman, judged that the landing could go on, and the flight director Gene Kranz gave the order to continue.

As Eagle came close to the ground, Armstrong saw that the computer was steering toward a field of boulders next to a large crater, later named West crater. He took over manual control and flew further on to find a clear spot. Eagle touched down in the Sea of Tranquility at 20:17 UTC with only about 25 seconds of descent fuel left before the crew would have had to abort. The landing point was roughly 6 kilometres beyond the planned target. Armstrong's first words from the surface were: "Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed." The capsule communicator who answered him was the astronaut Charles Duke.

## Walking on the Moon

The flight plan called for a rest period after landing, but the crew asked to start the moonwalk early. Armstrong stepped onto the surface at 02:56 UTC on July 21, 1969 and said, "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind." Aldrin followed about nineteen minutes later and described the view as "magnificent desolation". An estimated 600 million people watched the live television broadcast, which was then the largest audience for any broadcast in history.

The moonwalk lasted 2 hours and 31 minutes. The two astronauts planted a United States flag, spoke by radio-telephone with President Richard Nixon, and set up a small group of instruments called the Early Apollo Scientific Experiments Package. It held a passive seismometer, which measured moonquakes, and a laser ranging retroreflector, a panel of 100 fused-silica prisms that reflects laser beams sent from Earth. The retroreflector needs no power and is still used today to measure the distance to the Moon to within a few centimetres.

Armstrong and Aldrin collected 21.5 kilograms of rock and soil. The samples were mostly basalt and breccia. Scientists later found three minerals in them that had not been seen before: armalcolite, tranquillityite and pyroxferroite. Armalcolite was named after the three crew members, using the first letters of Armstrong, Aldrin and Collins.

They left behind a plaque fixed to a leg of the descent stage. It reads: "Here men from the planet Earth first set foot upon the Moon, July 1969 A.D. We came in peace for all mankind." They also left a small silicon disc carrying goodwill messages from the leaders of 73 countries, and a patch from Apollo 1 in memory of Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee, who died in a fire during a ground test in January 1967.

## Leaving the Moon and coming home

Eagle stayed on the surface for 21 hours and 36 minutes. While getting ready to leave, Aldrin found that the switch of a circuit breaker needed to arm the ascent engine had broken off. He pushed a felt-tip pen into the opening to close the circuit. The ascent stage lifted off, met Columbia in lunar orbit, and the two craft docked. After the crew and the sample boxes had moved across, Eagle's ascent stage was cast off and left in lunar orbit.

Columbia fired its engine to leave lunar orbit and headed home. It splashed down in the Pacific Ocean on July 24, 1969, about 1,500 kilometres south-west of Hawaii. The crew was picked up by a helicopter from the aircraft carrier USS Hornet, where President Nixon was waiting to greet them. The whole mission had lasted 8 days, 3 hours, 18 minutes and 35 seconds.

Because nobody could be sure that the Moon was free of harmful microbes, the astronauts put on biological isolation garments before leaving the capsule and were kept in quarantine for 21 days. They spent the first part of it in a converted trailer called the Mobile Quarantine Facility and the rest in the Lunar Receiving Laboratory in Houston. No lunar organisms were ever found, and quarantine was dropped after Apollo 14.

## The guidance computer

Both spacecraft carried an Apollo Guidance Computer, designed at the MIT Instrumentation Laboratory. It was one of the first computers built with integrated circuits. It had 2,048 words of erasable memory and 36,864 words of fixed memory. The fixed memory was called core rope memory because the program was literally woven into it: wires were threaded through or around small magnetic cores by workers at a Raytheon factory. The astronauts worked the computer through a keyboard and display unit known as the DSKY, typing commands as pairs of numbers called a verb and a noun.

The flight software was written by a team led by Margaret Hamilton. The software was built so that, when overloaded, it would restart and keep only the most important jobs running. This is what happened during the 1202 and 1201 alarms: the cause was a rendezvous radar switch left in a position that flooded the computer with useless signals, and the software kept the landing tasks alive while dropping the rest.

## What came after

Apollo 11 was followed by five more successful landings, ending with Apollo 17 in December 1972. In all, twelve people walked on the Moon. The command module Columbia is on display at the National Air and Space Museum in Washington, D.C. The exact place where Eagle's ascent stage ended up is not known; it was long thought to have crashed into the Moon, but a study published in 2021 suggested it may still be in orbit.
