# The Panama Canal

## Overview

The Panama Canal is a ship canal about 82 kilometres long that cuts across the Isthmus of Panama and joins the Atlantic Ocean to the Pacific Ocean. A ship sailing from New York to San Francisco through the canal travels about 9,500 kilometres. Going around Cape Horn at the tip of South America, the same trip is more than 22,000 kilometres. A typical passage through the canal takes between 8 and 10 hours.

Because of the way the isthmus bends, the canal does not run from east to west as many people expect. A ship going from the Atlantic to the Pacific actually travels toward the south-east, and the Pacific entrance lies further east than the Atlantic one.

## The French attempt

The first serious effort to dig a canal was French. It began in 1881 under Ferdinand de Lesseps, the diplomat who had led the building of the Suez Canal. De Lesseps wanted a canal at sea level with no locks, as at Suez. Panama was a very different place: the route crossed a ridge of hills, rain fell for most of the year, and the Chagres River flooded violently.

Disease proved worse than the terrain. Yellow fever and malaria killed workers in huge numbers, and the cause of the two diseases was not yet understood. An estimated 22,000 workers died during the French years. The company ran out of money and collapsed in 1889, wiping out the savings of hundreds of thousands of French investors. The scandal that followed led to fraud convictions for de Lesseps and for his son Charles, and also for Gustave Eiffel, the builder of the Eiffel Tower, who had been hired to design locks after the sea-level plan was dropped.

## How the United States took over

At the start of the twentieth century Panama was a province of Colombia. In 1903 the United States negotiated a canal treaty with Colombia, but the Colombian senate refused to approve it. Panama then declared its independence on November 3, 1903, with American warships standing by to keep Colombian troops away.

Fifteen days later the new country signed the Hay-Bunau-Varilla Treaty. The man who signed for Panama, Philippe Bunau-Varilla, was a French engineer and a shareholder in the failed canal company, and no Panamanian signed the treaty. It gave the United States control of a strip of land 10 miles wide, called the Canal Zone, in return for a payment of 10 million dollars and a yearly rent of 250,000 dollars. The United States also bought the equipment and diggings of the French company for 40 million dollars. American work began in 1904.

## Beating disease

The chief sanitary officer was an army doctor, William Gorgas. Work by Walter Reed's team in Cuba had recently confirmed that yellow fever is carried by one kind of mosquito, and it was already known that malaria is carried by another. Gorgas attacked the insects. His teams drained swamps, spread oil on standing water, fumigated houses, fitted screens to windows and built a piped water supply so that people no longer kept water in open containers. The last case of yellow fever in the Canal Zone was recorded in 1906. Malaria was never wiped out, but the number of cases fell sharply.

## The engineers

Three chief engineers led the American project. The first, John Findley Wallace, resigned after one year. The second, John Frank Stevens, was a railroad builder who served from 1905 to 1907. Stevens rebuilt the Panama Railroad so it could haul away the dug-out rock and soil, and he convinced President Theodore Roosevelt to give up the sea-level plan and build a canal with locks. When Stevens resigned, Roosevelt chose an army officer who could not quit: George Washington Goethals, who saw the canal through to the end.

## Design: a bridge of water

The canal does not cut through the land at sea level. It lifts ships up, carries them across, and lowers them again. The key to the design was the Gatun Dam, built across the Chagres River. The dam created Gatun Lake, which was the largest man-made lake in the world when it was filled. The surface of the lake lies 26 metres above sea level, and ships cross about 33 kilometres of the route on it.

Three sets of locks do the lifting and lowering. On the Atlantic side, the Gatun Locks raise a ship in three steps up to the lake. On the Pacific side, the Pedro Miguel Locks lower it by one step, and the Miraflores Locks lower it by two more steps to the level of the Pacific. Each original lock chamber is 110 feet wide and 1,000 feet long. The locks work by gravity alone, with no pumps: water flows down from the lake through large culverts to fill a chamber and then drains out toward the sea. Each passage of a ship through the original locks sends about 197 million litres of fresh water to the ocean.

Ships do not move through the original locks under their own power. They are guided by electric locomotives that run on tracks along the lock walls. The locomotives are called "mules", after the animals that once pulled barges on older canals.

## The Culebra Cut

The hardest digging was the Culebra Cut, a trench about 13 kilometres long through the ridge of the continental divide. The sides of the cut slid in again and again, and one slide could undo months of work. The cut was later renamed the Gaillard Cut after David du Bose Gaillard, the engineer in charge of that section, who died in 1913 before the canal was finished. In all, the American project dug out roughly 180 million cubic metres of material.

## Opening and cost

The canal opened to traffic on August 15, 1914. The first ship to make an official passage was the steamship SS Ancon. The opening got little attention in the world's newspapers because the First World War had begun in Europe less than two weeks before.

The American project cost about 375 million dollars, which made it the most expensive building project in United States history up to that time. About 5,600 workers died during the American period, from disease and from accidents. Most of the workforce came from the Caribbean, above all from the island of Barbados.

## From American to Panamanian control

Control of the Canal Zone by the United States was a cause of anger in Panama for many years, and riots in January 1964 left more than twenty people dead. In 1977 President Jimmy Carter and the Panamanian leader Omar Torrijos signed the Torrijos-Carter Treaties, which set out a step-by-step handover. Panama took full control at noon on December 31, 1999. Since then the canal has been run by a Panamanian government agency, the Panama Canal Authority.

## Ship sizes and the expansion

The size of the original locks set a limit on ship size that became known as Panamax. A Panamax ship can be at most 294.1 metres long and 32.3 metres wide. By the early 2000s many new container ships were too big to fit.

In a referendum held on October 22, 2006, Panamanian voters approved a plan to build a third, wider lane of locks. Work started in 2007 and cost about 5.25 billion dollars. The expanded canal opened on June 26, 2016, and the first ship through was the container ship COSCO Shipping Panama. The new locks are named Agua Clara, on the Atlantic side, and Cocoli, on the Pacific side. Their chambers are 427 metres long and 55 metres wide. Ships built to fit them are called Neopanamax and can be up to 366 metres long.

The new locks differ from the old ones in two ways. Ships are positioned by tugboats in place of locomotives. And next to each chamber there are water-saving basins that hold back and reuse about 60 percent of the water from each passage.

## Tolls and traffic

About 14,000 ships pass through the canal in a normal year. The toll depends on the type of ship, its size and its cargo, and the largest container ships pay more than a million dollars for one passage. The lowest toll ever charged was 36 cents. It was paid in 1928 by the American travel writer Richard Halliburton, who swam the length of the canal over ten days and was charged according to his weight.

The canal depends on rain. Gatun Lake supplies both the water for the locks and the drinking water for about half of Panama's people. During the drought of 2023 the canal authority had to cut the number of daily passages and limit how deep ships could sit in the water, and long queues of waiting ships formed at both entrances.
